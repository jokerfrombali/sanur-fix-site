Leak Under Your Kitchen Sink? Fix It Before It Spreads

A slow drip under the kitchen sink is easy to ignore until one humid Bali morning you open the cabinet and find a puddle on the tile floor. In most cases the culprit is a worn siphon gasket, a cracked flexible hose, or a loose fitting at the food-waste disposer. The good news: in five minutes with a torch and a dry cloth you can usually point to the exact source.

Where to Find the Leak Under Your Sink

Before you unscrew anything, shut off the two small quarter-turn valves (hot and cold) behind the sink. If your villa uses a rooftop tank fed by PDAM, the pressure can swing when neighbours run their showers, so a fitting that holds at low flow may weep at peak demand. Wipe every joint completely dry with a paper towel, then place a second dry towel on the cabinet floor as a catch-all.

Run the tap for thirty seconds, let it drain, and watch. The first wet spot tells you which zone to inspect: the siphon body, the inlet hoses, the disposer mount, or the dishwasher tailpipe. If you see no visible drip but the floor keeps getting damp, suspect a slow seep inside a wall-fed pipe; that one needs a professional with a camera.

  • Keep a small torch or phone light handy – Sanur cabinets are dark once the door closes.
  • Check the underside of the basin where the overflow channel meets the waste outlet; a hairline crack there is common in older villas.
  • If you smell gas near any appliance under the sink, stop working, open windows, and call a licensed technician immediately.

Siphon (P-Trap) and the Flexible Drain Hose

The siphon is the U-shaped pipe directly beneath the basin. In Bali's humid air the rubber or silicone washers at both ends lose elasticity faster than they would in a dry climate. Unscrew the slip-nut by hand (or with pliers wrapped in a rag), pull the trap out, and look for a flattened or torn washer. A new set of gaskets costs very little and takes two minutes to fit back on.

The flexible hose that runs from the top of the siphon into the wall or floor drain is the next suspect. Squeeze it gently along its length while water is running; a soft spot or a bead of moisture means the inner liner has split. Replace the whole hose rather than trying to patch it – a 60 cm replacement is a standard part.

  • Tighten slip-nuts finger-tight plus a quarter turn; over-torquing cracks the plastic body.
  • If your villa was built before 2010, check whether the wall outlet is still PVC or has been swapped to metal; mismatched threads are a classic leak point.
  • After reassembly, run hot water for a full minute to expand the seals, then wipe and re-check.

Flexible Supply Lines (Hot and Cold)

The braided stainless-steel hoses feeding the tap are under constant pressure, even when you are not using the sink. Sea air in Sanur accelerates corrosion at the compression fittings where the hose meets the valve and the basin tailpiece. Look for white mineral crust or a faint rust ring at either end.

To test: with the valves open, wrap a dry tissue around each connection and wait two minutes. If the tissue darkens, shut that side off, replace the hose, and re-test. Always buy a new hose rather than reusing one – the internal rubber liner degrades invisibly.

  • Never use Teflon tape on a compression fitting; it can pinch the washer and cause a worse leak later.
  • If the quarter-turn valve itself weeps from its body, the cartridge is worn. Replacing the whole valve is safer than chasing a drip behind the wall.
  • In villas with well-water systems, check for scale build-up inside the inlet screen; a blocked screen raises pressure on the hose.

Food-Waste Disposer (Garbage Disposal Unit)

The disposer sits between the basin waste outlet and the siphon. The most common failure points are the mounting-ring gasket (where the unit bolts to the sink) and the discharge-pipe seal into the drain line. Run water through the unit while you look at both joints; a slow ring of moisture means the rubber seal has hardened or been pinched during installation.

Also check the splash-guard O-ring inside the top opening. If food particles have worked their way under it, water bypasses the seal and drips onto the cabinet floor. Remove the guard, clean the channel, and replace the O-ring if it looks cracked.

  • Before touching any electrical connection near the disposer, confirm the breaker is off – water plus 230 V is not a combination to test.
  • Do not run the disposer dry; in low-flow PDAM periods this can overheat the motor and warp internal seals.
  • If the unit hums but does not spin, unplug it, remove the jam with an Allen key from the bottom, and only then re-test.

Dishwasher Connection

Many Sanur villas have a dishwasher tucked beside the sink. Its inlet valve sits on the cold-water supply, and its drain hose loops up before dropping into the siphon or wall drain. A kinked drain hose or a loose clamp at the siphon inlet will back-pressure water out of the joint every cycle.

Check the inlet-connection washer (a small rubber disc inside the nut) for flattening, and make sure the drain hose is clipped securely to the siphon's side port. If your dishwasher uses a dedicated branch tee rather than the siphon inlet, inspect that tee for hairline cracks – they are nearly invisible until they start weeping.

  • After any repair, run one full empty cycle and watch the cabinet floor for the first ten minutes of drainage.
  • Keep the air-gap loop above the flood line; in older villa layouts the hose sometimes drops below the rim, which invites siphoning.

Questions

How often should I replace the siphon washers in a Bali kitchen?

As a rule of thumb, inspect them every six months and replace the gasket set yearly if you notice any stiffness or discolouration. The tropical humidity and occasional PDAM pressure spikes age rubber faster than in temperate climates, so do not wait for a visible drip.

Can I use Teflon (PTFE) tape on my sink fittings?

Only on threaded metal-to-metal joints such as a wall valve connection. Never wrap it around compression nuts, slip-nuts, or plastic siphon threads – the tape can pinch the washer, distort the seal, and actually cause the leak you are trying to prevent.

The leak is inside the wall behind the sink. What now?

Shut off the main stopcock (usually near the meter or at the rooftop tank inlet), open the lowest tap to relieve pressure, and call a plumber who can access the pipe from above or through the floor below. Trying to chase a hidden-wall leak with DIY methods usually makes the damage worse before it is found.
