How to Test Your Pool Water: Strips, Drops & Digital Meters

Keeping your villa pool clear in Sanur means more than adding chlorine on a schedule. The salt air off the beach, the humidity during rainy season, and the mineral content of PDAM or well water all shift your chemistry faster than you would expect. Testing regularly is the single most important habit for protecting swimmers, tiles, and pump seals.

Test Strips – Quick and Cheap

Dip-and-read strips are the easiest starting point. You dip the pad into a cup of pool water, wait about fifteen seconds, and compare the colour against the chart printed on the bottle cap. They give you pH, free chlorine, total alkalinity, and sometimes cyanuric acid in one go.

The trade-off is accuracy. In Bali's heat, the reagents on the strip can degrade if the bottle sits in a hot garage or under direct sun. Store them in a cool drawer, not on the pool deck. If two colours look close together, trust the lower reading rather than guessing upward.

  • Use a clean plastic cup; never dip the strip straight into the pool.
  • Swirl gently for five seconds so the water fully wets the pad.
  • Read within thirty seconds before the colours bleed.
  • Replace the strip bottle every three to four months, even if it looks full.

Drop (Liquid) Test Kits – More Precise

A drop kit uses small vials and liquid reagents: one for pH, one for free chlorine, one for alkalinity, and often one for calcium hardness. You add a measured number of drops, swirl, and read the colour change against a printed scale.

Because the reagents are liquids in sealed bottles, they stay stable longer than strips, which matters when your villa is unoccupied for a week or two during the dry season. The main downside is that you need a separate vial per parameter, so testing all four takes a few extra minutes.

One safety note: keep chlorine reagent away from any other chemical container. If you ever smell a sharp, choking gas while mixing products near the pool, stop immediately, ventilate the area, and call a professional. Never pour one chemical into another to 'save time'.

Electronic (Digital) Testers

A digital meter reads pH and sometimes ORP (oxidation-reduction potential) with a probe you lower into the water. No colour-matching under Bali's harsh midday sun, no guessing between two shades on a faded chart.

They are convenient if you test daily, but the probes need regular calibration with buffer solutions, usually every month or after heavy use. A drifting reading can be more misleading than an honest strip result. If your meter starts showing values that do not match a quick strip check, recalibrate before trusting it again.

What You Should Actually Measure

Four numbers matter most for a residential villa pool in Sanur:

pH – target 7.2 to 7.6. Outside this range chlorine becomes less effective and skin gets irritated. Rainy-season runoff from the roof tank can push pH up; salt air can nudge it down.

Free chlorine – keep it between 1 and 3 ppm. Below 1, algae finds a foothold. Above 5, swimmers get stinging eyes and the smell turns sharp.

Total alkalinity – aim for 80 to 120 ppm. This is your pH stabiliser. PDAM water in parts of Sanur tends to carry higher carbonate levels, so alkalinity can creep upward over weeks.

Cyanuric acid (stabiliser) – 30 to 50 ppm protects chlorine from UV breakdown under the tropical sun. Too high and chlorine stops working even when the test reads fine.

How Often to Test

If you swim daily or have children using the pool every morning, check pH and free chlorine once a day with strips or a digital meter. Run the full four-parameter drop test at least twice a week.

During rainy season (roughly November to March), increase frequency. Heavy rain dilutes chlorine, adds organic load from leaves on the roof tank, and shifts alkalinity. A quick strip check after any downpour above an hour is worth doing before anyone gets in the water.

Even if the villa sits empty for a fortnight, test all parameters the day you return. Stagnant warm water in a Bali climate can swing chemistry dramatically in ten days.

Questions

My pool water looks clear but my eyes sting. What should I check first?

Start with free chlorine and pH. Clear water does not guarantee safe chemistry. If pH is above 7.8, chlorine efficiency drops sharply even when the level reads normal. Adjust pH back into the 7.2–7.6 range, wait a few hours, then retest chlorine before swimming again.

Can I use PDAM tap water to top up my Sanur pool without testing?

PDAM water varies by neighbourhood and season. It may carry higher alkalinity or residual disinfectants that interact with your pool's stabiliser. After a large top-up (more than half a tank), run a full drop test within twenty-four hours rather than assuming the old readings still hold.

I mixed two different algaecides and got a strong chemical smell. Is this dangerous?

Stop what you are doing, step away from the pool area, and ventilate. Do not add any further chemicals until the air clears. If you experience coughing, eye burning, or dizziness, move to fresh air immediately and seek medical help.
