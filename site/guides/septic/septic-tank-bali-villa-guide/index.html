A typical Bali villa does not connect to a municipal sewer line. Wastewater from your kitchen, bathrooms, and laundry flows into an underground septic system that settles solids and lets the liquid percolate into the surrounding soil. In Sanur, where many properties sit close to the water table and rely on PDAM or well water, knowing how your septic works can save you from costly surprises during the November-to-March rainy season.

How a Septic Tank Actually Works

At its simplest, a septic tank is a buried concrete or plastic box with two jobs: separate solids from liquids, and give the liquid time to break down before it reaches the ground. Wastewater enters through an inlet pipe, usually positioned near the kitchen or main bathroom stack. Heavier material sinks to form a sludge layer at the bottom; lighter grease and oils float as a scum layer on top. The middle band of relatively clear water exits through an outlet pipe into a soakaway trench or drain field, where it slowly filters through sand and clay.

In older Sanur villas built in the 1990s or earlier, the construction is often very basic: a single rectangular concrete pit with a simple gravel bed behind it. There may be no baffle walls, no second chamber, and the access lid might just be a slab of concrete set flush with the lawn. That still functions, but it needs more frequent attention than a modern two- or three-chamber system.

The Main Types You Will Find in Bali

Most properties in Sanur fall into one of four categories. A single-chamber concrete tank with a gravel soakaway is the most common in older villas along Jalan Raya and the smaller side streets. Two-chamber tanks add a settling stage before the effluent leaves, which reduces clogging downstream. Newer builds sometimes use moulded fibreglass or polyethylene tanks that are lighter to install and easier to pump out. Finally, low-lying plots near the beachfront may use a small submersible pump to lift effluent up to a higher drain field, because gravity alone cannot carry the water far enough.

Whichever type you have, the principle is the same: settle, break down, percolate. The differences matter when you are planning maintenance or dealing with a blockage.

  • Single-chamber concrete pit – simplest, oldest design
  • Two- or three-chamber tank – better separation, less frequent pumping
  • Fibreglass / plastic tank – modern build, easy access for vacuum trucks
  • Pump-assisted system – used where the ground level makes gravity flow impossible

Sizing: Is Your Tank Big Enough?

Tank volume is usually matched to the number of bedrooms and bathrooms in the villa. A two-bedroom house with one bathroom might get away with a single 1,000-litre chamber; a four-bedroom villa with an outdoor kitchen and laundry will need considerably more. If you rent out the property on short stays, the daily water load jumps sharply compared with a single family living there year-round.

A few Sanur-specific points worth checking. Pool backwash water should never be directed into the septic inlet; it carries chlorine and algaecide that kill the bacteria doing the breakdown work. Rooftop rainwater tanks overflow separately and should stay separate from the wastewater line. And if your villa draws from a well rather than PDAM, the higher mineral content can accelerate scale buildup inside pipes feeding the tank.

Where Is It Buried on Your Property?

In most Sanur villas the tank sits somewhere under the garden, along the rear boundary wall, or tucked behind the pool deck. Look for a round concrete ring set slightly proud of the ground – that is your access lid. In older properties the lid may be hidden under a patch of grass, a small frangipani tree, or even a thin layer of laterite soil that has shifted over the years.

If you cannot find it, ask the previous owner or the original builder for a rough location sketch. Many long-term tenants in Sanur have inherited a villa without ever seeing the tank. A quick walk around the perimeter with a metal probe (a long screwdriver works) will usually reveal the lid within ten minutes. Once you know where it is, mark it clearly so landscapers or pool contractors do not dig through it during renovations.

Common Problems and What to Do About Them

The most frequent issue is sludge buildup. Over time the solid layer at the bottom thickens until it chokes the outlet pipe. You will notice slow-draining toilets, gurgling sounds in the kitchen sink, or a faint sewage smell near the garden. The fix is straightforward: a vacuum truck pumps out the sludge and scum layers. How often this is needed depends on household size and water use; there is no fixed schedule, but if you see the signs above, book a pump-out before an overflow happens.

Root intrusion is the second big problem, especially in older concrete tanks where hairline cracks let fig or banyan roots grow into the chamber. During the rainy season (roughly November to March), heavy rainfall can also saturate the soakaway bed faster than it drains, causing wastewater to back up toward your lowest bathroom. If you ever detect a strong rotten-egg or sulphur gas smell inside the villa, stop using all taps and toilets immediately, ventilate the area by opening windows, and call a professional. Hydrogen sulphide in enclosed spaces is dangerous, and attempting to open a sealed tank yourself risks a worse exposure.

One more safety note: never pour chlorine-based pool chemicals, drain cleaners, or bleach directly down a toilet that feeds the septic. Mixing concentrated chlorine with organic waste can produce toxic fumes and destroy the bacterial culture the system relies on.   • Slow drainage or gurgling pipes – likely sludge buildup
  • Sewage odour near the garden – check for cracked lid seal or root damage
  • Overflow after heavy rain – soakaway may be saturated; avoid running large volumes of water until it recovers
  • Strong H₂S / rotten-egg smell indoors – evacuate the room, ventilate, call a professional

Questions

How often should I have my septic tank pumped out?

There is no universal interval. It depends on how many people use the villa daily, whether you run a short-term rental, and the tank's original size. As a rule of thumb, if your drains start slowing or you notice an odour, book a pump-out. A technician can inspect the sludge level through the access lid and tell you exactly when the next service is due.

Can I pour pool backwash water into my septic system?

No. Pool water contains chlorine, algaecide, and stabilisers that will kill the anaerobic bacteria responsible for breaking down waste in the tank. Direct backwash to the stormwater drain or a designated soakaway away from the septic inlet. If you are unsure where your drainage lines go, send a photo of your plumbing layout on WhatsApp and we will point you in the right direction.

My toilet keeps backing up after heavy rain. Is it the septic?

It could be. In Sanur's rainy season the ground around the soakaway bed saturates quickly, so effluent cannot percolate as fast as usual and backs up toward the lowest fixture. It may also be a partial blockage in the pipe between the bathroom stack and the tank. A quick inspection of the access lid and a pressure test on the line will identify which one it is.
