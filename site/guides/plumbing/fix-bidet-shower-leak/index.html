Bidet Shower Leaking? How to Stop a Dripping Hygiene Hose

You've turned off the tap, but water still trickles from that little hand-held sprayer bolted next to your toilet. In Sanur's salty air and hard PDAM or well water, the thin brass fittings on a budget hygiene kit corrode faster than you'd expect. The good news is that most of these leaks come down to one perished rubber washer or a split in the flexible hose, not a full plumbing job.

How a Hygiene Shower Actually Works

A typical bidet-shower kit has three parts: a small diverter valve (a ball-valve or ceramic-disc cartridge) screwed into the main cold-water tap, a flexible metal-reinforced hose about one to one-and-a-half metres long, and a hand-held spray head with a rubber gasket inside. Water flows through the diverter, down the hose, and out the face of the head when you squeeze the trigger.

In many older Sanur villas this unit was added after construction as an aftermarket add-on, often threaded onto a tap that was never designed for it. That means the connection point is a weak link from day one.

Leak at the Hose or Spray Head

The most common failure I see here is a hairline split in the hose right where the metal crimp meets the plastic fitting. The sea air accelerates corrosion on those thin brass sleeves, and once a crack appears, water seeps along the braid and drips onto the tile floor every time you use the shower above.

Inside the spray head, the little rubber O-ring that seals against the hose nipple dries out and shrinks within a year or two, especially if the bathroom has no ventilation fan running during the dry season's heat cycle. You'll notice a slow drip even when the trigger isn't squeezed.

Mineral deposits from PDAM or well water also clog the tiny spray holes, so the stream turns into a weak trickle or stops altogether while the body of the head still holds pressure behind the blockage.

  • Squeeze the hose gently along its length; any damp spot marks the split.
  • Unscrew the head and look for a flattened or cracked rubber gasket inside.
  • Hold the head up to light – white crusty build-up means limescale is blocking the face.

Leak at the Connection Valve

If water weeps from where the hose meets the tap body, the culprit is usually the internal washer on the diverter valve. It's a thin fibre or rubber disc that compresses against a seat every time you open the flow. After months of hard-water cycling it thins out and can no longer seal fully.

In older villas I've also found the tap threads themselves pitted with rust, so even a new washer won't sit flat. If the metal around the connection looks orange-brown or crumbly, don't keep tightening – the thread will strip and you'll end up replacing the whole tap stub-out.

A small drip here may seem minor, but over a Sanur rainy season it can soak the grout line behind your toilet and start staining the wall plaster within a few weeks.

Replacing It Yourself – Step by Step

Before you touch anything, shut off the water supply. In most Sanur villas there's an isolation ball-valve under the sink or in the utility room; if you're on PDAM with a rooftop tank, close the tank inlet as well so gravity doesn't keep pushing water through the pipe while you work.

Place a towel and a shallow bowl under the connection. Unscrew the hose from both ends (tap side and head side) using two adjustable wrenches to avoid stripping the hex flats. Wipe away old PTFE tape residue with a rag.

Inspect every thread for corrosion. Wrap fresh PTFE tape clockwise on clean threads – about four turns is plenty. If the diverter valve feels gritty when you turn it, replace the whole cartridge rather than just the washer.

Screw the new hose on hand-tight first, then give one quarter-turn with a wrench. Don't over-torque plastic fittings; they crack. Reassemble the spray head, making sure the rubber gasket sits in its groove before you tighten.

Open the supply slowly and check each joint for weeping. Run the sprayer for thirty seconds to flush any debris. If everything stays dry after ten minutes of use, you're done.

  • Keep a bucket ready – residual pressure will squirt out the moment you break the seal.
  • If there's an electrical outlet or light switch within arm's reach of your working area, make sure it's switched off at the breaker first. Water plus live electrics near a bathroom floor is not something to gamble with.
  • Use only PTFE (white) plumber's tape. Rubber washers are fine on the valve seat, but don't mix them with tape on the same thread.

What to Choose Instead of the Cheapest Kit

The budget hygiene-shower sets sold in local hardware shops look identical on the shelf, but the brass is thin, the seals are generic, and the hose braid is barely two layers of wire. In Sanur's humidity they tend to fail within six to twelve months, and by then the tap threads are often damaged too.

When you do replace the kit, look for a solid-brass or stainless-steel body with a ceramic-disc cartridge rather than a fibre washer. A braided stainless hose with a 316-grade fitting will shrug off sea air far better than plated zinc. The spray head should have a removable face plate so you can soak out limescale without replacing the whole unit.

Questions

Can I seal a split hose with epoxy glue or heat-shrink tubing?

No. The hose carries pressure every time you open the tap, and any adhesive joint will fail under that cyclic load, usually within days. Replace the hose section or the full hose; it's a small part and the safest fix.

Why does my bidet shower work fine in June but start dripping by September?

The dry-season heat expands and contracts the metal fittings, while the rainy season adds constant humidity that accelerates corrosion on thin brass. The combination weakens seals gradually, so the leak often appears right as the weather shifts.

Do I really need a plumber for this, or can I manage it myself?

If it's just a worn washer or a split hose, the steps above should cover it. But if the tap body itself is corroded, the threads are stripped, or water has already seeped behind your wall tiles, call a professional before you make things worse.
