Common Villa Guest Complaints in Sanur & How to Fix Them

Most negative reviews for Sanur villas trace back to five things: lukewarm showers, weak tap pressure, a septic-tank smell, a green pool, or a generator that will not stop. None of them are mysterious. Each one has a mechanical cause you can catch during a routine check-in walk-through, long before your next guest notices.

Hot Water That Never Gets Hot Enough

In Sanur most villas heat water with either a gas instant heater or an electric geyser fed from a rooftop tank. The usual culprit behind 'the shower is warm but never hot' is limescale building up inside the heat exchanger. PDAM mains water and many well supplies here carry enough calcium to coat the element within a year or two, especially if the tank sits unused between bookings.

Before a guest arrives, run every tap and shower for two minutes and watch the temperature climb. If it plateaus below what the dial says it should reach, the scale layer is thickening. A technician can descale the exchanger or replace the sacrificial anode rod in a geysers. In the rainy season, outdoor gas units also collect moisture; make sure the rain cap is intact and the ignition lead is dry.

  • Run all showers at full flow for 60 seconds once a week while the villa is occupied.
  • Check the gas-heater flame colour: steady blue is fine; yellow or flickering means call a professional before anyone else uses it.
  • If you smell gas at any point, stop using the unit, open windows, and call your plumber immediately—do not flip electrical switches near the leak.

Weak Pressure That Makes Guests Grumble

PDAM supply in Sanur fluctuates through the day, and older villas often have narrow galvanized pipes that have corroded from the inside. Add sea-air salt on outdoor fittings and you get partial blockages that show up as 'the water is so weak' in a review.

Start at the source: check the strainer screen where the PDAM line enters the rooftop tank. A mesh clogged with sand or rust will halve your pressure without any visible leak. Then walk the pipe run to the furthest tap; if only one bathroom is affected, the local valve or mixer cartridge is likely worn rather than the whole system.

  • Clean inlet strainers every two months, more often during dry-season dust spikes.
  • Listen for a hammering knock when taps close—that is water hammer and will eventually burst a joint in an old villa's pipework.
  • If the problem appears after a PDAM outage, flush the tank before reconnecting to avoid pushing sediment into the lines.

That Sewage or Musty Smell Guests Cannot Ignore

Bali villas almost always sit on septic tanks, and the tropical heat accelerates bacterial growth in the sludge layer. When guests say 'there is a smell coming from the toilet even after flushing,' the tank is usually due for pumping or a vent-pipe blockage is trapping gases under the slab.

A second common source is the rooftop tank itself. If it has held still water for weeks between bookings, biofilm forms on the walls and releases a musty odour that travels down the cold-water pipes. Drain and rinse the tank, then refill before the next check-in. Also check that every floor drain trap still holds water; a dried-out P-trap lets sewer gas straight into the room.

  • Pump the septic tank at least once every 18 months of continuous use; more often if you have three or more bedrooms occupied daily.
  • Pour a cup of water into each shower-drain and sink trap after a vacancy so the seal re-forms.
  • If the smell is strong and persistent, do not open manhole covers yourself—call your technician to inspect the vent line safely.

Pool Problems That Show Up in Photos

Sanur's sea breeze carries salt onto pump seals, ladders and the filter housing, while the rainy season dilutes chlorine and stirs up algae spores from the bottom. A pool that looked fine in March can turn cloudy-green by June if nobody tops up chemicals after a week of rain.

Test free chlorine and pH twice a week during high season and at least weekly when the villa is between guests. Keep the cover on during storms so leaf litter does not feed bacteria. If you are topping up with granular chlorine, add it to running water only—never pour dry chemical directly into the skimmer basket.

  • Never mix chlorine tablets with any other product (algaecide, shock, acid). If fumes sting your eyes or throat, step back, ventilate, and call a professional before touching anything else.
  • Inspect the pump O-ring for salt-crystal pitting every three months; a weeping seal will flood the motor box.
  • After heavy rain, re-test within 48 hours because dilution drops chlorine below safe levels fast.

Noise Guests Hear Through Thin Walls

Sanur compounds are close together, and older villas were rarely built with acoustic insulation around mechanical rooms. A generator idling at night, a pool pump vibrating on bare concrete, or an AC compressor cycling hard all read as 'unacceptable noise' in a review even if the decibel level is moderate.

The fix is usually mechanical rather than cosmetic: mount pumps on rubber anti-vibration pads, check that the generator exhaust is routed away from guest-facing windows, and make sure the AC condenser fan is not rubbing its housing. Water-hammer knocks in old pipework can also sound like a loud banging at 2 a.m.; a pressure-reducing valve near the tank inlet often quiets it down.

  • Walk the property at night during your own stay to hear what a guest would hear.
  • Tighten any loose pipe brackets you find; rattling copper or PVC against a wall frame amplifies every flow change.
  • If the generator trips repeatedly under load, do not keep restarting it—have the fuel line and carburettor checked before the next booking.

How to React When a Guest Actually Complains

Stay calm and ask three questions: which room, what were they doing (shower, tap, pool), and when did it start? Then ask them to take a short video of the issue with their phone—running water, the gauge reading, the smell source if visible. That clip saves everyone time later.

Send the photos and video to your technician on WhatsApp along with the villa address and a one-line description. Do not attempt gas-line repairs, electrical work near wet surfaces, or chemical mixing yourself. A five-minute misjudgement can turn into a flooded bathroom or a chlorine-gas incident that ends up in a very public review.

Once the fix is done, walk through the affected area yourself before the guest returns. A quick 'I've had this sorted and tested' message goes a long way toward turning a 3-star draft into a 5-star edit.

Questions

How often should I service my villa's hot-water system in Sanur?

At minimum once a year, check the anode rod, heat-exchanger scale, and gas-ignition lead. If you draw from a well or use PDAM without a softener, inspect every six months because limescale builds faster here than in most climates.

My pool turned cloudy after the rainy season—what do I test first?

Check free chlorine and pH within 48 hours of heavy rain. Rain dilutes both; if chlorine drops below 1 ppm and pH drifts above 7.6, algae will take hold quickly. Top up gradually, brush the walls, and run the filter for at least eight continuous hours before adding any shock treatment.

A guest reports a sewage smell but the toilet flushes fine. Should I let them stay?

First identify the source: is it one bathroom or the whole house? Check floor-drain traps and the vent-pipe opening on the roof. If the odour is strong, persistent, and coming from under the slab, have your technician inspect the septic tank level and vent line before the next booking. In the meantime, keep windows open and avoid running the kitchen disposal.
