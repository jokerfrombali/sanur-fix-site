Water Around Your Pool: Causes and Fixes for Sanur Villas

If you see puddles forming on the deck or grass ring around your pool after rain or backwashing, it is not just a cosmetic issue. In Sanur's humid climate and heavy monsoon months, that standing water quietly washes fine soil out from under the concrete slab. Left unchecked, the ground beneath your pool can shift, crack tiles, and eventually pull the whole structure down.

Why Water Pools Around Your Pool

The most common cause is simple: the ground was never graded away from the pool edge. Many older villas in Sanur were built with a flat pad and no channel drain, so every drop of overflow, backwash discharge, or monsoon rain just sits there. After a few weeks the surface looks damp; after a few months the soil underneath has been carried away.

Other frequent culprits include a blocked corner drain, a backwash hose that dumps directly onto the lawn instead of into a soakaway, a leaking rooftop tank pipe running along the pool wall, or PDAM pressure surges forcing water through a cracked supply line near the pump room. Sea-spray salt also accelerates corrosion on metal fittings, which then weep small amounts of water day after day.

  • Check whether the deck slopes at least 1–2 cm per metre away from the pool coping.
  • Trace where the backwash discharge actually ends up – it should go to a soakaway pit, not the garden bed.
  • Look for rust streaks on the skimmer box, return jets, and any exposed pipework.

The Real Risk: Soil Erosion Under the Slab

When water seeps under a concrete slab it does not just sit there. It picks up fine sand and silt and carries them out through any crack or joint. Over time this creates a void beneath what looked like solid ground. You will first notice a soft spot when you step on the tile, then a hairline crack in the grout, and eventually a visible dip or collapsed section.

In Sanur's rainy season (roughly November to March) a single afternoon downpour can deliver more water than surrounding soil can absorb in days. If your pool sits on uncompacted fill – common in older villa builds near the beach road – erosion happens faster. The sea breeze keeps everything damp, so the wet-dry-wet cycle weakens the sub-base continuously.

  • A soft or spongy feeling underfoot near the pool edge is the earliest warning sign.
  • Cracks in deck tiles but not in the pool shell usually point to ground movement below.
  • If tapping the slab with a mallet sounds hollow, there may already be a void.

Getting Drainage Right

The fix starts with moving water away from the structure, not covering it up. A properly sized channel drain running along the long side of the pool, connected to a soakaway pit at least two metres from any wall, handles most overflow and backwash discharge. In tight villa plots where space is limited, a French drain laid beneath a strip of decorative gravel works well and stays out of sight.

If the problem is a leaking supply line or rooftop tank pipe, isolate and replace that section before doing any drainage work. Otherwise you keep feeding the same hole with fresh water every day. For PDAM connections, fitting a pressure-reducing valve on the main inlet protects both pipework and joints around the pump room.

  • Slope surrounding ground so water flows toward a drain, never toward the pool base.
  • Route backwash discharge into a dedicated soakaway, not onto lawn or flower beds.
  • Keep electrical sockets and the pump panel at least one metre above the splash zone; if water reaches them, stop using the equipment and call an electrician.

What Repair Actually Looks Like

A typical repair goes like this: identify the exact source – a cracked pipe, failed joint, blocked drain – and stop it. Then carefully excavate the affected area, remove washed-out soil, and replace it with compacted crushed stone or graded fill. If the slab has dropped more than about five millimetres, we may need to lift and re-bed that section, or inject fast-setting grout under the tile from access points.

The surface is then restored with new grout, matching tile replacement, or a thin self-levelling screed over the repaired patch. Because every villa differs – some have reinforced concrete decks, others just poured sand-cement – the scope varies a lot. The best way to gauge what is involved is to send clear photos of the wet spot, any cracks, and the general layout on WhatsApp; the technician reviews them and quotes for the specific job.

  • Do not pour extra cement over a sunken area without addressing the void underneath; it will crack again within weeks.
  • After any excavation near the pool, check the waterproofing membrane before backfilling.
  • Allow at least 48 hours of curing time before walking on freshly grouted tiles in Bali's heat.

A Simple Monthly Check

You do not need special tools to catch this early. Once a month, walk the perimeter after a rainstorm or backwash cycle with a flashlight. Look for damp patches, rust stains, or soil washed into the grass ring. Tap deck tiles with the heel of your hand; a hollow note means air below. If you notice any of these, take a photo and reach out before the next monsoon front rolls in.

  • Clear leaves and debris from corner drains every two weeks, especially during rainy months.
  • Inspect the backwash hose connection for splits or loose clamps each filter-clean.
  • Wipe down the skimmer box and return jets monthly to spot early corrosion from sea air.
  • If you smell gas near the pump room or see water pooling around electrical fittings, stop using the equipment immediately and call a licensed professional.

Questions

How do I know if soil is already eroding under my pool?

The first sign is usually a soft or spongy feeling when you step on tile near the edge, followed by a hairline crack in the grout that was not there before. If tapping the slab produces a dull, hollow sound instead of a solid thud, an air pocket is likely forming underneath. At that stage it is still straightforward to fix – send us a photo and we will advise.

Can I just pour cement over the sunken area and be done?

No. Cement poured over a void will simply crack again within a few weeks because nothing solid supports it beneath. You need to excavate, replace the lost soil with compacted fill, and then restore the surface properly. It takes longer but actually lasts.

Does backwashing really cause this much damage?

It can, if the discharge hose dumps 50–80 litres per minute directly onto uncompacted garden soil for several minutes every week. Over a year that is thousands of litres washing fine particles out from under your deck. The fix is simple: route the hose into a soakaway pit or drainage channel so water disperses harmlessly away from the structure.
