A wobbly toilet or a slow drip under the bowl is more than an annoyance in a Sanur villa – over time, salt air and humidity can turn a small leak into real floor damage. Whether you are swapping out a cracked unit that has survived years of PDAM pressure spikes or fitting a new one in a freshly renovated bathroom, the process follows the same core steps. Here is what actually matters when getting it done right the first time.

When It Is Time to Replace

You do not need a dramatic crack to know a toilet has had its day. Persistent wobbling that returns every few weeks despite re-tightening the anchor bolts, a hairline fracture near the base, or a seepage ring at the porcelain-to-floor junction that reappears after every reseal all point to the same conclusion: the unit or its connection needs replacing.

In older Sanur villas built before the last renovation wave, the original rough-in was sometimes quick work. The drain flange may have shifted, the floor slab underneath can be slightly uneven, and the wax ring from installation day has long since crumbled. If your current toilet is ten-plus years old and you keep chasing the same drip, a full replacement will save you repeated patch jobs.

Preparing the Floor and Drain Opening

Before anything new goes down, the old unit comes up – carefully. Support the bowl with a second pair of hands (or a small jack) so you do not yank the drain pipe out of the wall or slab. Once it is off, shut the water supply valve and let the tank empty completely.

Now inspect what is underneath. In sea-facing bathrooms the salt-laden air accelerates deterioration of any organic material, so check the subfloor for softness or delamination. Clear debris, old wax, and mineral build-up from the drain opening. Confirm the floor is level; some older villas have a slight slope that will make a new toilet rock no matter how tight you bolt it.

If your villa runs on a well pump or a rooftop gravity tank, make sure the supply line to this bathroom is isolated before you start working. A surprise burst at the connection while the bowl is lifted can flood the tile below in seconds.

  • Shut the isolation valve and flush until the tank is dry
  • Lift the old unit straight up; do not twist
  • Scrape all old wax or gasket residue from the flange
  • Check the flange bolts are still biting into solid substrate

Flange, Gasket, and Seal Choice

The critical interface is where the toilet outlet meets the drain pipe. Traditionally this is sealed with a wax ring, but in Bali's tropical humidity wax degrades noticeably faster than in drier climates – it softens, cracks, and loses its compression within a couple of years.

A quality rubber or EPDM gasket holds its shape far longer in 80 percent-plus ambient humidity. Seat the new toilet firmly onto the gasket without forcing or rocking it side to side; that just distorts the seal. If the flange itself is cracked, loose, or sitting too low (a common issue when the original install was rushed), replace or shim it before you set the bowl down.

Anchor the toilet through the two mounting holes into the flange bolts. Snug them evenly – enough to stop movement, not so tight that you crack the porcelain base.

Sealing Around the Base

Once the unit is bolted and level, apply a thin, even bead of flexible silicone sealant around the perimeter where the porcelain meets the tile. Flexible matters: rigid epoxy will crack as the building settles or the floor flexes under foot traffic, and then water wicks underneath.

Leave the rear gap slightly open rather than sealing it completely. That small opening lets you spot any slow weep at the drain connection during the first few days instead of discovering a puddle under the tank three weeks later.

During the rainy season, moisture gets everywhere in an unsealed bathroom. A proper silicone ring stops capillary action from pulling water under the tile grout line, which is especially important on upper-floor bathrooms where there is a ceiling below.

Checking After Installation

Run four or five full flushes in a row and watch the base closely for any darkening of the silicone or a faint damp ring. Then check the supply-line connection at the fill valve for drips – a slow drip here can look identical to a base leak but has a different fix.

Sit down gently (or press down with both hands) to confirm the unit does not rock. If it still moves after 24 hours, the problem is almost never the bolts; it is flange height, an uneven subfloor, or a gasket that was compressed unevenly when the bowl was set down.

Re-inspect the base the next morning. Sometimes a very slow seep only becomes visible once the sealant has fully cured and the building has settled overnight. If you see a fresh damp spot, lift the toilet, replace the gasket, and re-seat before the moisture reaches the slab.

Questions

My toilet wobbles even though I tightened the anchor bolts. What is going on?

Tightening bolts more will eventually crack the porcelain without fixing the movement. The usual culprits are a shifted or corroded flange, a soft spot in the subfloor, or a gasket that was compressed unevenly during install. The correct fix is to lift the unit, inspect and repair the flange and floor, then re-seat properly.

There is a small drip under my toilet that keeps coming back. Should I just add more silicone?

Silicone hides the symptom but does not stop the leak. In almost every case the water is escaping at the wax ring or rubber gasket where the bowl meets the drain pipe. You need to lift the toilet, remove the old gasket, fit a new one, and reseal. Patching over it only delays the problem and risks rotting the subfloor.

Can I install a replacement toilet myself in my Sanur villa?

If you are comfortable with basic plumbing and the existing flange is solid and level, it is doable. However, many older villas here have rough-in work that was done quickly years ago – misaligned drains, low flanges, or cracked anchor points. A local technician can assess from photos on WhatsApp and save you a second visit plus potential water damage to the tile below.
