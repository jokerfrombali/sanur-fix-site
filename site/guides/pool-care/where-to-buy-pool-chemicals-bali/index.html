If your villa pool in Sanur is turning green or smelling off, you probably need fresh chlorine, pH adjuster, and maybe an algaecide. You do not have to fly back to Europe or Australia to sort this out. Here is what to buy, where to get it locally, and how to avoid wasting money on counterfeit products.

What Your Pool Actually Needs

Most Sanur villa pools are small to medium (6–12 m long) and sit outdoors with full sun exposure for most of the year. That combination means chlorine burns off quickly and algae finds any opening. The core kit is simple:

Keep these four items on hand and test your water weekly. If your villa uses well water instead of PDAM mains, the mineral balance can shift more often, so you may need to dose pH down or alkalinity buffer a little more frequently. Pools within a few hundred metres of the beach also pick up salt spray from the sea breeze, which adds a slow corrosive load on tiles and metal fittings.

  • Granular chlorine or TBT tablets – your primary sanitizer
  • pH Down (sodium bisulfate) and pH Up (soda ash) – keep pH between 7.2 and 7.6
  • Alkalinity buffer (soda ash / baking soda grade) – stabilises pH swings
  • Liquid algaecide – useful before rainy season or after heavy leaf fall
  • Clarifier – clears cloudy water after a chemical shock

Where to Buy Pool Chemicals in Bali

You have three practical options depending on how much you need and how quickly.

Small hardware and building-material shops along Jl. Raya Sanur and the side streets toward Batu Bolong stock basic granular chlorine, tablets, and pH adjusters. Walk in, point at the product, and pay cash or transfer. Selection is limited but prices are straightforward.

Larger building-supply depots in Denpasar (the area around Jl. Gatot Subroto and Jl. Teuku Umar) carry a wider range, including branded liquid algaecides and clarifiers. A short drive from Sanur gets you there in ten minutes if traffic allows.

Many pool technicians and villa managers in Sanur also sell chemicals directly through WhatsApp. You send them your pool size and current test-strip readings; they recommend a quantity and deliver within a day or two. This is often the easiest route for expats who prefer not to navigate a market.

Prices – What to Expect

Costs vary by brand, pack size, and whether you buy retail or in bulk. A small bag of granular chlorine will cost less than a 20 kg industrial sack, obviously. Liquid products in 5-litre jugs sit somewhere in between.

I do not publish fixed price lists because suppliers change their rates with import costs and exchange-rate swings. If you are unsure what a fair price looks like for your specific product, send a photo of the label on WhatsApp and I can tell you whether it seems reasonable before you pay.

One tip: buying a month's supply at once usually works out cheaper per kilogram than topping up every two weeks. Just make sure you store it properly (see below).

Spotting Counterfeit Chemicals

Bali's open import channels mean that off-brand or mislabelled pool chemicals occasionally reach local shops. They may look fine on the shelf but under-dose, meaning your water stays slightly green and you keep adding more, which is both wasteful and irritating.

A few red flags to check before you pay:

If you ever mix two different chemical containers – say, pouring algaecide into an open bucket of concentrated acid – stop immediately, ventilate the area, and call a professional. Chlorine compounds reacting with acids release toxic gas. Do not try to 'test' a reaction yourself.

  • Blurred or missing batch/expiry dates on the label
  • Granules that are the wrong colour for what the label claims (e.g., pale yellow instead of white for trichlor)
  • Plastic packaging that feels thin, crinkly, or has no heat-seal at the top
  • No manufacturer address or only a generic 'Made in Indonesia' line with no company name

Stocking Up Before Rainy Season

Bali's wet season runs roughly from November through April. In Sanur it is less dramatic than in Uluwatu, but you will still get days of heavy rain that dump leaves, mud, and organic matter straight into your pool. Algae blooms spike within 48 hours if chlorine levels drop.

Practical routine: two weeks before the first sustained rains, do a full chemical shock, clean the filter, and top up your algaecide. Then keep a two-to-three-month supply of chlorine tablets and pH adjuster stored in a cool, dry cupboard away from direct sun. Do not store chemicals next to each other – keep oxidisers separate from acids.

If your villa relies on a rooftop tank fed by PDAM mains, check the tank lid and inlet screen during storms. A cracked lid lets debris in, and dirty water entering the pool system throws off your chemistry faster.

Questions

Can I just use table salt instead of chlorine for my Sanur pool?

Saltwater systems work, but they need a dedicated salt-chlorine generator cell installed in the pump circuit. Retrofitting one into an older villa pool is a plumbing job, not a DIY swap. If you are happy with standard chlorine dosing, stick with granules or TBT tablets – simpler and cheaper to maintain.

How often should I test my pool water in Bali's climate?

Once a week minimum, more often (every 2–3 days) during hot dry months when evaporation concentrates minerals, and after any heavy rain. Use liquid test kits rather than strips if you want accuracy; strips give a rough guide only.

My pool water tastes metallic. Is that normal with well water?

It can happen if iron or manganese levels in the well are high. It is not dangerous at typical concentrations, but it will stain tiles over time. A simple whole-house filter before the pool feed line usually solves it.
