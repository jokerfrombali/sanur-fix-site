If your sliding door or window has started sticking, dragging along the track, or refusing to latch shut, the culprit is almost always worn rollers or a degraded rubber seal. In Sanur's salty sea air and monsoon humidity, both degrade noticeably faster than in a dry inland climate. The good news: most of these fixes are straightforward once you know exactly what to look at.

What Goes Wrong with Sliding Doors

A very common issue with sliding doors here is that the panel used to glide smoothly but now requires a firm push, or it drops slightly so the bottom edge scrapes the floor. Other signs include a rattle when you open it, water seeping through during the rainy season, and a lock that no longer meets its strike plate cleanly.

In older Sanur villas built before 2010, the aluminum frames were often fitted with basic nylon rollers and thin EPDM seals. After ten or twelve years of salt-laden air and UV exposure, those parts simply wear out. It is not a structural problem; it is a consumable-parts issue.

Rollers: The First Thing to Check

Lift the sliding panel by about two centimetres and look underneath. You will see one or two small wheels on each side, usually mounted in a bracket at the bottom of the frame. If they are cracked, flattened, or covered in a gritty white residue (that is salt corrosion), they need replacing.

To swap them: remove the retaining screw or clip, slide the old roller out, fit the new one, and re-secure. Most replacement rollers are available from hardware shops around Sanur or Legian. Match the diameter and axle type exactly; a half-millimetre difference changes how the panel sits in the track.

  • Clean the track with a soft brush and a damp cloth before testing; trapped sand and dust can mimic roller failure.
  • Apply a thin coat of silicone spray to the new rollers after fitting—avoid petroleum-based grease, which attracts more grime in humid air.
  • If the roller bracket itself is bent or corroded through, replace the whole bracket assembly rather than forcing a new wheel onto a warped arm.

Rubber Seals and Gaskets

The rubber strip that runs around the perimeter of the sliding panel keeps rain and insects out. In Bali's climate it hardens, cracks, and eventually sheds small pieces into the track. Press your thumb along the seal: if it feels brittle, has visible splits, or leaves a dark mark on your skin, it is time for a new one.

Removal is simple—most seals just press-fit into a groove. Pop the old strip out section by section, measure its cross-section (common sizes are 6 × 4 mm or 8 × 5 mm), and buy a matching D-profile or P-profile replacement. Seat the new seal firmly so there are no gaps at the corners.

  • During the dry season (May–September) UV damage accelerates; check seals every six months.
  • A light wipe with a damp cloth monthly slows salt buildup on the rubber surface.
  • Do not use silicone sealant to 'fix' a cracked gasket—it will only make the next removal harder.

Locks and Latches

A sticking door often drags the lock off alignment. The bolt may miss the strike plate by a few millimetres, so you end up forcing the handle down. Before replacing any hardware, fix the rollers and seals first; once the panel hangs level again, the lock usually re-engages correctly.

If the lock still feels stiff after that, remove the two screws on the faceplate, clean the bolt mechanism with a dry brush, and apply a tiny amount of graphite powder. Avoid liquid oil inside the lock body—humidity will turn it into a sticky sludge within weeks.

Adjusting the Frame and Track

Many sliding doors have small adjustment screws at the top or bottom of the frame that let you raise or lower the panel by a couple of millimetres. Turn them clockwise to lift; counter-clockwise to drop. Make one-quarter-turn adjustments and test the slide after each change.

Also check that the track is level. A slight tilt (common in older villas where the concrete slab has shifted) means the panel always drifts to one side. If the tilt is more than about three millimetres across the full width, the track itself may need shimming or the frame re-anchored—that is a job best left to a technician rather than a DIY fix.

  • Wipe the track clean every month during the rainy season; standing water plus salt air corrodes aluminum quickly.
  • Keep the area around the door free of pool chemicals and chlorine splashes if your villa sits near a pool deck.
  • After any roller or seal replacement, run the door through ten full open-close cycles before declaring it done.

Questions

How often should I replace rollers on a Sanur sliding door?

There is no fixed schedule—it depends on usage and exposure. As a rule of thumb, inspect them every year and expect replacement somewhere between five and eight years for standard nylon rollers in coastal air. If you hear grinding or see flat spots, do not wait.

Can I use WD-40 on the track and rollers?

No. WD-40 is a displacer and light lubricant, not a long-term grease. In Bali's humidity it evaporates quickly and leaves behind a film that grabs dust and sand. Use a silicone-based spray instead, applied sparingly once or twice a year.

My sliding window leaks rainwater even though the seal looks fine. What else could it be?

Check the drainage holes at the bottom of the frame—clogged weep holes are the most common cause. Clear them with a thin wire or compressed air. Also make sure the panel is sitting level; if one side sits lower than the other, water will pool and find its way past the seal.
