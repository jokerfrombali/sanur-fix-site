Pool Painting in Sanur: A Practical Guide

Your pool paint does not simply fade; under Bali's UV load and salt-laden sea air it cracks, chips, and turns chalky within a few dry seasons. In Sanur, where every open villa sits metres from the Indian Ocean, a poorly prepped coat can start peeling before the next rainy season even begins. The good news is that a proper strip-and-repaint, done at the right time of year, gives you years of smooth, clean water again.

When You Actually Need a Repaint

Walk the pool edge with your hand (gloves on) after a thorough brush-down. If the surface feels rough like fine sandpaper, if you see hairline cracks running through the old coat, or if white chalky patches appear despite regular brushing, the top layer has lost its bond. Water staining that soaks through to the concrete below means the film is no longer acting as a barrier.

Timing matters in Sanur. The driest stretch runs roughly May through September, giving you long, low-humidity days for curing. Scheduling during peak rain (November to April) is risky: a single afternoon downpour can ruin a fresh epoxy coat before it cross-links. Aim for at least four consecutive dry days around your application window.

  • Chalky or powdery patches when you rub the wall with a damp cloth
  • Hairline cracks wider than about 0.5 mm across the floor or walls
  • Persistent greenish or brown tint that survives a full acid shock and scrub
  • Rough texture that catches on bare skin or makes tile edges feel jagged

Choosing the Right Paint for a Tropical Pool

Most villa pools in Sanur are gunite or shotcrete basins, sometimes lined with an older acrylic or rubberised coating from the 1990s build era. For these substrates, a two-part epoxy system is the standard choice: it bonds mechanically into the porous concrete, resists the mineral content of PDAM tap water, and shrugs off the salt spray that drifts inland every afternoon.

Polyurethane topcoats add extra UV resistance and a slightly softer feel underfoot, which some owners prefer for kids' pools. Acrylic paints are cheaper but thinner; they tend to wear faster in high-traffic areas like steps and ladders. Your technician will look at the existing substrate, the pool's orientation (north-facing walls dry slower), and how often you run the filter before recommending a specific system. The final quote depends on the job scope, so send photos first and get a written estimate.

Preparation: Where Most Repaints Fail

Roughly seventy percent of a successful repaint happens before any paint touches the wall. Start by draining the pool completely—this also lets you inspect the structure for leaks while the basin is empty. Pressure-wash every surface with a fan-tip nozzle to strip loose old paint, algae film, and calcium scale.

Next comes acid etching. A dilute muriatic or phosphoric acid wash opens up the concrete pores so the new epoxy can grip. This step produces fumes; work early in the morning with the villa doors open, wear goggles and a respirator, and never mix the acid with any chlorine-based product already sitting in the bucket. If you smell strong fumes or feel your eyes sting, stop immediately and ventilate.

Patch every crack wider than 1 mm with a polymer-modified cement compound, feathering it flush with the surrounding surface. Sand the whole basin with 40- then 80-grit paper until you have a uniform, slightly toothy texture. Finish with a dedicated epoxy primer that matches the topcoat system. Skip the primer and you will see delamination within a season.

  • Drain fully and pressure-wash at 2,500–3,000 psi
  • Acid-etch, rinse thoroughly, let dry 24 hours minimum
  • Fill cracks > 1 mm with patching compound; sand smooth
  • Apply one even coat of compatible primer; allow full cure per data sheet

Applying the Topcoat

Epoxy pool paints are typically applied with an airless spray for large flat walls and a roller for corners, steps, and the waterline band where brush marks would collect dirt. Two to three coats is standard; each coat needs the drying window printed on the tin before the next goes on—rushing this traps solvent and causes soft spots.

Keep the pool covered with a fitted tarp during curing so Sanur's fine red dust, pollen from the frangipani trees, and stray insects do not land in the wet film. Avoid running the backwash cycle or adding any algaecide until the manufacturer's full-cure period (often 72 hours) has passed. If you notice runs, sags, or a cloudy haze after the first coat dries, lightly scuff-sand and recoat rather than trying to 'blend it out' with a second pass over a defective base.

How Long the Finish Will Last

A well-prepped epoxy system on a sound gunite basin typically holds up for three to five years in Sanur's conditions before showing visible wear at high-traffic points like the deep end wall and step nosings. Salt air, relentless UV, and the constant chemical load from chlorine or salt-cell systems all work against the film.

You can stretch that window by keeping your filter running at least eight hours a day, brushing walls weekly, and topping off with fresh water rather than letting the level drop below the skimmer lip. A thin refresh coat every two years—no need to strip everything—keeps the surface sealed and looking new far longer than waiting for full failure.

Questions

Can I just paint over my existing pool coating without stripping?

Only if the old layer is fully adhered, free of chalky spots, and chemically compatible with the new product. If you see any peeling, softness, or solvent smell when you rub it, you must sand back to bare concrete first. Painting over a failing base guarantees early delamination, especially in Bali's humidity swings.

What happens if it rains while the epoxy is still curing?

Water sitting on an uncured epoxy coat prevents cross-linking, leaving a soft, tacky surface that will never reach its designed hardness. You would need to grind the affected area back down and re-prime before recoating. This is why scheduling around Sanur's dry season and checking the forecast for at least four clear days is essential.

Do I have to drain the whole pool, or can I repaint just one wall?

A partial repaint of a single wall or the waterline band is possible if the rest of the basin is in good shape. You still need to lower the water level well below the work area, mask off adjacent surfaces, and ensure the patch blends into the existing colour. For full-basin repainting, a complete drain is necessary so every surface dries evenly before priming.
