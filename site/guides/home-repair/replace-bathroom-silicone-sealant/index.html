Bathroom silicone doesn't last forever—especially in Sanur's salt-laden sea breeze and monsoon humidity. If your shower corners are blackening, peeling, or letting water sneak behind the tiles, a fresh bead of sealant will sort it out quickly. Below is exactly when to re-caulk, how to remove the old stuff without damaging tile, and which product actually survives here.

When It's Time to Replace

The most obvious sign is mould that no amount of scrubbing will lift. Once black spots have worked into the body of the silicone rather than sitting on the surface, the bead is done. Other red flags: edges lifting away from the tile, a crumbly or chalky texture, visible gaps where you can see daylight through the joint, or a musty smell even after cleaning.

In Sanur the combination of coastal salt air and six months of heavy rain accelerates all of this. Older villas built before the mid-2010s often still carry their original caulk, which may be well past its useful life even if it looks superficially fine. A good habit is to run a fingernail along every joint twice a year—once just before the rainy season and once after. If the edge lifts or feels spongy, plan a re-caulk.

  • Mould stains that penetrate below the surface
  • Edges peeling or separating from tile or glass
  • Crumbling, cracking, or a powdery feel when touched
  • Water seeping behind tiles or into wall cavities
  • A persistent damp smell in the shower area

Removing the Old Silicone

You don't need fancy equipment. A sharp utility knife or a dedicated silicone-removal blade, a plastic scraper, and a cloth soaked in white vinegar or 70 % isopropyl alcohol will do the job. Work with the window open or an extractor fan running; cutting old sealant releases small particles you'd rather not breathe.

Score one long side of the bead with the blade held at a shallow angle so you cut the silicone without scoring the tile. Once one side is free, grip the strip and peel it away in long sections rather than tearing it into chunks—this keeps residue to a minimum. Scrape off any remaining film with the plastic tool, then wipe the joint thoroughly with your solvent-soaked cloth until the tile looks glossy and the joint feels smooth under your fingertip. Let the surface dry completely before moving on.

  • Wear safety glasses and work gloves
  • Cut along one edge first, then peel in strips
  • Scrape residual film with a plastic (not metal) scraper
  • Degrease with vinegar or isopropyl alcohol
  • Allow the joint to air-dry fully—no shortcuts here

Applying the New Bead

Stick painter's tape along both sides of the joint for clean edges. Cut the cartridge nozzle at roughly 45 degrees, opening about 6 mm wide for a standard shower corner. Load the gun, squeeze out a small blob onto a scrap piece of cardboard to clear air from the tube, then start at one end of the joint.

Pull the trigger steadily and move the nozzle in one continuous pass—pausing mid-joint creates a weak spot where water will eventually find its way in. Once the bead is laid, run a wet finger or a silicone-smoothing tool along it to press it into the corner and give it a rounded profile. Peel off the masking tape immediately while the sealant is still tacky; waiting even ten minutes can leave ragged edges. Keep the area dry for at least 24 hours before the first shower.

  • One smooth, unbroken pass per joint
  • Smooth with a damp finger right after laying
  • Remove tape while the bead is still wet
  • No water contact for a full 24-hour cure

Choosing the Right Sealant

For any bathroom joint that sees regular water, go with 100 % silicone rather than a silicone-acrylic blend. The blends look identical on the shelf but lack the flexibility and water resistance of pure silicone, and they tend to fail within a year or two in a humid climate.

Two curing chemistries are common: acetoxy (acetic acid) and neutral-cure. Acetoxy gives a strong bond and a sharp vinegar smell during cure—fine for tile-to-tile joints. Neutral-cure is gentler on painted surfaces, mirrors, and certain plastics, so it's the safer pick if your joint touches a painted wall or a glass panel. In Sanur's humidity, choose a product labelled mould-resistant or anti-fungal; the added biocide keeps black spots at bay noticeably longer between cleanings.

Caring for Your Fresh Sealant

The single best habit is to squeegee or wipe the shower walls after each use so soap film and hard-water residue don't build up on the bead. During the rainy season, when you're likely taking more showers, this matters even more because the constant moisture gives mould spores every chance to settle.

Once a month, give the joints a quick wipe with a cloth dampened in diluted white vinegar. If you spot early grey or black specks, treat them immediately with a small amount of hydrogen peroxide applied directly to the silicone. Catching contamination early means a wipe fixes it; letting it sit for weeks means the spores have penetrated the body of the sealant and that section needs re-caulking rather than cleaning.

Questions

How often should I re-caulk my bathroom in Sanur?

There's no fixed schedule, but inspect every six months—ideally just before and just after the wet season. Salt air and high humidity here degrade silicone faster than in drier regions, so plan on refreshing the bead sooner rather than later if you see any lifting or discolouration.

Can I paint over old silicone instead of replacing it?

No. Paint won't bond properly to cured silicone and will peel off within weeks, especially once water gets behind it. The only reliable fix is to remove the old bead and apply fresh, compatible sealant.

Do I need to shut off the water supply before re-caulking?

Not necessarily, as long as the joint you're working on stays completely dry during removal and application. Just make sure there's no active leak spraying onto the area and that the surface has fully dried before you lay the new bead.
