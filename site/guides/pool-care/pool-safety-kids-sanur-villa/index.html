Pool Safety for Kids in Your Sanur Villa

A villa pool in Sanur is a joy, but an unsupervised child near open water is the single biggest risk most families face here. The good news: a few physical barriers, a proper cover, and clear house rules dramatically cut that danger. Below are the things I check when I service pools around Sanur and what I would tell any parent before their first hot season here.

The Real Risks Around a Villa Pool

Older villas in Sanur were often built with open-plan layouts where the kitchen flows straight into the garden and then the pool area, sometimes with nothing more than a change of tile between them. There is no door, no threshold, no natural barrier. A toddler who wanders out looking for a snack can reach the water's edge in three steps.

The tropical heat makes kids want to be in or near the water almost all day. Add the rainy season, when a heavy afternoon downpour can raise the pool level by several centimetres in twenty minutes, and you have a situation where the water line creeps closer to the coping stone while nobody is watching.

Sea air also plays a quiet role. Salt-laden breeze corrodes metal gate hinges, weakens plastic latches, and degrades rubber seals on covers over a year or two. Something that locked perfectly last dry season may hang loose now. Worth checking every month.

  • Check that every path from indoor rooms to the pool has at least one closed-door or gated checkpoint.
  • Walk the perimeter after each storm; look for shifted stones, cracked tiles, or a raised waterline.
  • Test gate latches and self-closers monthly – salt corrosion is sneaky.

Fencing and Barriers That Actually Work

If your villa does not already have a dedicated pool room with a self-closing door, the single most effective upgrade is a physical barrier between the living area and the pool deck. It does not need to be a full commercial fence. A removable mesh panel (the kind sold as a baby-gate extension) across the narrowest gap, combined with a self-closing gate on any existing doorway, closes off the main access route.

Whatever barrier you choose, make sure it faces away from the pool so a child cannot simply push open and walk in. Keep chairs, planters, and toys well clear of the fence line; kids use them as steps. If your villa has a glass sliding wall overlooking the pool, confirm the lock actually engages – many older Sanur villas have worn rollers that let the panel drift open a few centimetres, just enough for a small child to squeeze through.

  • Self-closing, self-latching gate positioned so it swings away from the water.
  • No climbable furniture within arm's reach of any barrier.
  • Glass walls: test the lock weekly; replace worn rollers before they fail completely.

Pool Covers and Safety Nets

A proper safety cover or a fine-mesh net stretched over the pool adds a second layer of protection when the pool is not in use. In the rainy season this also keeps out leaf litter and excess rainwater, which dilutes your chlorine and can push the level above the skimmer. That matters because a higher waterline means less dry coping stone for a child to grip if they slip.

Inspect the cover every two weeks. UV exposure and salt air break down mesh fibres faster than you would expect. A small tear that looks harmless today can widen into a hole large enough for a hand or a foot by next week. If you see fraying, do not wait – get it repaired or replaced.

One important rule: never let children stand, sit, or play on the cover as though it were a trampoline. Even a rated safety net is designed to support weight slowly distributed, not to take a jumping impact.

  • Check mesh integrity after every heavy storm and at least bi-weekly in dry season.
  • Store spare chemical tablets away from the cover storage area (see below).

Storing and Handling Pool Chemicals Safely

Chlorine tablets, algaecide, pH adjusters – these are the chemicals I handle most often around Sanur pools. They work well, but they are caustic and irritating. Keep them in their original labelled containers, stored in a locked cabinet or a high shelf well out of a child's reach. The tropical humidity accelerates degradation, so check expiry dates and discard anything that has crumbled, discoloured, or smells strongly of chlorine fumes.

If you ever need to mix or dose chemicals and something goes wrong – unexpected fumes, a reaction, a splash near your eyes – stop what you are doing, move to fresh air, rinse with plenty of water, and call a professional for guidance before trying another product. Never combine different pool chemicals unless the label explicitly says they are compatible; mixing certain compounds can release toxic gas. When in doubt, do not mix it yourself.

  • Locked storage, original labels intact, no loose tablets on the deck.
  • Keep a bucket of clean water nearby when dosing, in case of skin contact.
  • Strong chlorine smell indoors? Ventilate immediately, get everyone outside, then call for help.

Simple Daily Rules Every Kid Should Know

You cannot fence away every risk, so pair the physical setup with habits. A few rules repeated daily become second nature: no running on wet stone, always an adult within arm's reach for children under six, and a life ring or throw-rope kept at the poolside at all times rather than locked in a shed where nobody remembers it is there.

Teach older kids one critical skill: if someone falls in the water, they shout for a grown-up and grab the rope – they do NOT jump in after them. This single instruction prevents most well-meaning secondary drownings. Post the local emergency number (118) on the wall near the pool and make sure your phone has it saved.

Finally, brief every new nanny, au-pair, or visiting relative on the first day. 'The pool door stays closed' sounds obvious until you have a grandparent who keeps opening it to hang laundry out by the water.

  • Life ring / throw rope at the pool edge, accessible without unlocking anything.
  • Emergency numbers posted visibly and saved in every family member's phone.
  • Brief all helpers and visitors on pool-door policy before their first shift or visit.

Questions

Do I still need a barrier if my pool sits inside a walled compound?

Yes. The outer wall stops strangers but does nothing to stop a child who walks out of the kitchen. You want at least one gated or self-closing checkpoint between any indoor room and the pool deck, regardless of how secure the perimeter fence is.

Can a safety net replace a solid cover entirely?

A fine-mesh net is a good second layer and handles rain and debris well, but it is not a substitute for supervision. It slows a fall-in, it does not prevent one. Use it as part of your setup alongside fencing and clear rules, not as the only protection.

What should I do immediately if a child gets into the pool unattended?

Stay calm, call 118 (Bali emergency) from another person if possible, and get the child out safely – use the throw rope rather than jumping in yourself unless you are a strong swimmer. Rinse any chemical contact with clean water, keep the child warm, and seek medical attention even if they seem fine, because aspiration can cause delayed symptoms.
