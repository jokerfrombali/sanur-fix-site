Why Is My Pool Water Cloudy? Causes and Solutions

Cloudy or milky-white pool water almost always means microscopic particles are floating in your pool because something upstream has failed – chemistry, filtration, or both. In Sanur, constant sea-salt spray, nearby construction dust, and hard PDAM or well water make this a very common complaint. The good news is that most cases can be cleared without draining the entire pool.

What Actually Causes Cloudy Pool Water

The most frequent culprits in Sanur villas are organic load (sunscreen, sweat, leaves from frangipani trees), mineral buildup from hard tap or bore-well water, and fine silt that enters during the rainy season when gutters overflow into the pool area. Older villas built before modern plumbing standards sometimes have corroded copper fittings that leach tiny metal particles into the system.

Sea air is another quiet factor. Salt crystals settle on surfaces, dissolve back into the water, and over weeks raise the dissolved-solids level until the water looks faintly white even though chlorine readings appear normal.

  • Sunscreen and body oils from swimmers
  • Leaves, pollen, and insect debris
  • Hard-water minerals (calcium, magnesium) from PDAM or wells
  • Silt and dust blown in by trade winds
  • Corroding old plumbing in pre-2000 villas

pH and Chlorine: The Chemistry Check

Before you reach for any product, test your pH and free chlorine with fresh strips or a digital meter. In Bali's heat and humidity, chlorine breaks down faster than in cooler climates – a reading of 3 ppm at 9 am can drop to 1 ppm by afternoon. If pH drifts above 7.8, chlorine becomes far less effective, and the fine particles it would normally keep in suspension start to cloud the water.

PDAM water in Sanur tends to be slightly alkaline, so if you top up frequently without adjusting, pH creeps up week after week. Well water varies more; some boreholes deliver very soft, acidic water that swings the other way. Either extreme makes the pool look hazy even when everything else is clean.

  • Ideal pH range: 7.2 – 7.6
  • Free chlorine target: 1 – 3 ppm (higher during peak season)
  • Test every morning before adding chemicals
  • Adjust pH first, then dose chlorine – never the reverse

Is Your Filter Keeping Up?

Many older Sanur villas were fitted with sand filters sized for a smaller pool, or the pump simply runs fewer hours per day than the manufacturer recommends. When the filter cannot capture particles below about 20 microns, they recirculate endlessly and the water takes on a milky cast.

During the rainy season (roughly November to March), gutters and overflow drains dump silt straight into the skimmer. If you have not backwashed the sand filter in two or three weeks, it will clog and lose pressure. A quick visual check: if the pressure gauge reads well above normal, or the return jets feel weak, the filter media needs cleaning or replacing.

  • Backwash a sand filter every 1–2 weeks in dry season, weekly in wet season
  • Run the pump at least 8 hours daily in hot months
  • Inspect and clean the pre-filter basket monthly
  • Replace cartridge or sand media when backwashing no longer restores clarity

Coagulant: The Quick Clarity Fix

A coagulant (sometimes sold as 'clarifier' or 'flocculant') works by giving those tiny suspended particles an electrical charge so they clump together into larger flakes. Once the flakes are big enough – roughly 50 microns – your existing filter catches them on the next cycle instead of letting them swirl around.

It is safe for swimmers once the water has been filtered through, but do not add coagulant at the same time as algaecide or shock chlorine; let each product finish its job first. In practice, one dose followed by 6–8 hours of continuous filtration clears most mildly cloudy pools within a day or two. If the water is still hazy after 48 hours, the root cause is probably upstream (chemistry or filter) rather than just particle size.

  • Dose according to pool volume – check the label
  • Run the pump continuously for 6–8 hours after dosing
  • Do not mix with shock chlorine in the same hour
  • Vacuum to waste if large floc settles on the bottom

When It's Time to Drain and Refill

If you have corrected pH, cleaned the filter, used coagulant, and the water is still cloudy after a full week, the dissolved-solids level (TDS) has likely crept too high. This happens when a pool is topped up repeatedly without ever being drained, especially with hard PDAM water. At that point no amount of chemistry will restore clarity; the only real fix is a partial or full drain and refill with fresh water.

A rough rule of thumb: if your TDS reading exceeds about 1,500 ppm, plan a drain cycle. In Sanur, where sea air adds extra salts, many villa pools benefit from a top-up-and-drain every 12–18 months even if they look fine on the surface.

Questions

Why does my pool turn white right after I add chlorine?

That milky film is usually calcium carbonate precipitating out because the pH was too high when you shocked. Let it run for 24 hours, re-test pH, and bring it back into the 7.2–7.6 range before adding more chlorine. If the haze persists beyond two days, the issue is suspended particles rather than a chemical reaction.

Can I swim while coagulant is working in the pool?

Once the product is fully dissolved and the floc has been filtered through (typically 6–8 hours of pump runtime), the water is safe to swim in. Avoid swimming during the active clumping phase, and never mix coagulant with shock chlorine or algaecide in the same hour – let each finish its job first.

How often should I change the water in a Sanur villa pool?

There is no fixed schedule, but in Sanur's salty, humid air most pools accumulate dissolved solids faster than inland pools. Check TDS every few months; if it passes roughly 1,500 ppm or the water stays cloudy despite correct chemistry and filtration, plan a drain-and-refill cycle. For many villas that works out to once every 12–18 months.
