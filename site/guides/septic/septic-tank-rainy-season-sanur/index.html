Sewer & Septic Problems in Sanur's Rainy Season

When the first really heavy rains hit Sanur, many villa owners notice slow drains, gurgling pipes, or worse – grey water backing up into the bathroom. Most of these problems are not mysterious; they come from rainwater mixing with your wastewater and overwhelming a small septic tank that was never designed for the extra volume. Here is what actually happens under your garden and how to stop it before the worst downpour arrives.

What Goes Wrong When It Rains Hard

Sanur gets its heaviest rainfall between November and March. In a single afternoon you can see 60–90 mm drop on the roof, and all of that water has to go somewhere. In older villas along Jalan Raya Sanur and the smaller lanes behind it, the original plumbing was often laid in the 1980s or 90s with clay or early PVC joints that have now cracked or shifted. Rainwater seeps through those gaps and floods the sewage line from above.

The most common symptoms within 24 hours of a storm are: the toilet flushes slowly or not at all, the shower drain bubbles and smells, and a faint brownish tint appears in the lowest point of the house – usually the ground-floor bathroom or the kitchen sink. If you see actual sewage pooling near the septic chamber lid in your garden, the tank has already overflowed.

How Stormwater Sneaks Into Your Sewer Line

In many Sanur properties the rain gutter system discharges straight into the same underground pipe that carries grey water and toilet waste. That is a design shortcut builders took years ago, and it means every heavy shower literally pours extra litres into your septic tank. Add PDAM tap-water use (showers, washing machine) on top of that, and the tank fills far faster than normal.

Check where your gutters terminate. Follow the downpipe to its outlet. If it connects to a round manhole cover labelled 'sewer' rather than a square storm-drain grate, you have a combined system. In that case the fix is as simple as rerouting one downpipe to a soakaway pit or the street-side storm channel. A small plumbing job, but it removes most of the rainy-season pressure from your tank.

  • Trace every roof downpipe to its final outlet before the first wet month.
  • Make sure the kitchen sink P-trap and bathroom drains are not cross-connected to the storm line – this is common in older builds.
  • If you collect rooftop water for garden irrigation, keep that tank's overflow separate from the sewer pipe.

What Happens Inside the Septic Tank

A typical Sanur villa has a two-chamber concrete or plastic tank buried under the lawn or carport. It relies on anaerobic bacteria to break down solids while the liquid layer flows out through a perforated pipe into the soil. When a sudden slug of rainwater arrives, it dilutes the bacterial sludge and pushes the liquid level up faster than the drain field can absorb it. The result: the tank overflows back toward the house.

You will notice the smell change first – a sweet, rotten-egg odour near the chamber lid even though nothing is visibly leaking. That means the liquid surface is at or above the inlet pipe. If you open the lid (wear rubber gloves; the fumes are unpleasant) and see a thick grey foam instead of clear-ish water with a scum layer, the bacteria have been washed out and the tank needs a full pump-out before it works properly again.

Protecting Your System Before the Wet Season

The best time to act is October, when the sky is still mostly blue but the forecast is turning. A 30-minute walk around your property catches most problems early:

Clear leaves and palm fronds from gutters and downpipes so water actually flows off the roof instead of spilling over the edge onto the ground beside the septic chamber. Check that the soil around the tank lid is not compacted by foot traffic or a parked car; loose, well-drained soil helps the drain field breathe. If your villa has a check valve on the main sewer line, make sure it is not stuck shut – a simple push-test with a stick tells you. Finally, if it has been more than two years since the last pump-out, schedule one now rather than waiting for an emergency call in December.

If any of these checks reveal a cracked pipe, a missing manhole cover, or a tank that smells strongly even after pumping, send photos through WhatsApp and get a quote before the rains start. The price depends entirely on what needs replacing and how accessible the line is under your garden.

Emergency Steps When Sewage Backs Up

Stop using all water fixtures immediately – no flushing, no shower, no washing machine. Every litre you add pushes more waste toward the lowest point of the house. If the backup is in the bathroom, place towels to contain the spread and open windows for ventilation; do not use a wet-dry vacuum near any electrical outlet or GFCI socket without first confirming the circuit is off at the breaker box.

Do not mix household bleach or chlorine tablets into standing sewage 'to kill the smell.' Chlorine kills the bacteria your tank still needs and can create irritating fumes in a confined space. If you detect a strong gas odour (hydrogen sulphide) or see brown water rising past the toilet rim, leave the room, ventilate, and call a licensed plumber or septic service. A professional will pump the tank, clear the blockage, and check the drain field while it is still manageable rather than after the whole garden is flooded.

Keep a note of where exactly the water appeared, roughly how much, and whether it was grey (bathroom/kitchen) or black (toilet). That information helps the technician diagnose faster when they arrive.

Questions

Why does my septic tank smell worse right after rain?

Rainwater dilutes the bacterial layer that normally keeps solids breaking down quietly. The liquid level rises, the scum layer gets disturbed, and hydrogen-sulphide gases escape through any small gap in the lid seal. Once the tank settles over two to three days the smell usually fades; if it persists, the tank likely needs pumping.

Can I just let the extra rainwater sit in the tank until it drains out?

In a well-sized system with a healthy drain field, a moderate amount of extra water will percolate within a day or two. But Sanur's soil can become saturated during consecutive rainy weeks, so the drain field slows dramatically. If you see standing water around the chamber lid for more than 48 hours, call for a pump-out rather than waiting.

Do I need to replace my whole sewer line every few years because of the salt air?

Not necessarily. PVC and modern HDPE pipes handle Sanur's coastal humidity fine for decades. Problems usually come from old clay joints, tree roots entering a crack, or ground settlement under older foundations. A camera inspection (the technician quotes after seeing photos of your setup on WhatsApp) tells you exactly which section, if any, actually needs work.
