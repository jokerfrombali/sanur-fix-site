Black & Mustard Algae in Your Pool: How to Get Rid of It

Black algae shows up as dark specks or streaks on tiles and walls, while mustard (yellow-green) algae forms a fuzzy film that can reappear within hours of treatment. Both are common in Sanur villas, especially after the rainy season when organic matter washes into the water. The good news is that with the right approach you can clear them without damaging your pool surface.

How to Identify Black vs. Mustard Algae

Start by running your finger across the affected area. Black algae feels gritty and almost embedded in the tile; if you scrape lightly it leaves a dark residue behind. Mustard algae slides off more easily and has a soft, slimy texture. It often appears yellowish-green rather than pure black, which is why locals sometimes call it 'horchich' algae.

In older Sanur villas, black algae loves shaded corners near the skimmer, step edges, and any rough patch where plaster has chipped. Mustard algae tends to spread across flat surfaces—pool floor, steps, the waterline—especially where circulation is weak.

  • Black algae: dark specks, gritty, hard to scrub, usually in shaded or rough areas
  • Mustard algae: fuzzy yellow-green film, slides off easily, spreads fast on flat surfaces
  • If both appear at once, treat the mustard first—it dies faster and makes the black easier to target

Why They're So Resistant to Chlorine

Both types build a protective biofilm—a slimy layer of organic compounds that sits between the algal cells and your chlorine. A standard shock dose can't penetrate that shield, so the algae looks dead on the surface but regrows from living cells underneath within 24–72 hours.

Sanur adds extra challenges. The constant sea breeze carries salt and organic particles into open pools. PDAM tap water and well water here vary in mineral content, which can shift pH and give algae a foothold. During the rainy season (roughly November to March), leaf litter, bird droppings, and construction dust wash straight into the basin if the cover isn't tight.

Removing the Algae Step by Step

Before you start, check your pH (aim for 7.2–7.6) and free chlorine level. If either is way off, correct them first—algicide won't work properly in unbalanced water.

For mustard algae: brush the area firmly with a stiff pool brush, apply an algicide formulated for yellow-green algae per the product label, let it sit for the stated contact time, then run the filter continuously for at least eight hours. Re-test chlorine the next morning; a second dose is often needed.

Black algae needs more patience. Scrub the spots with a dedicated tile brush or non-metallic scraper until you see fresh plaster. Apply a concentrated algaecide directly onto the wet surface, let it dwell for the full recommended time, then shock the pool. Repeat every two to three days for up to a week. Never mix different chemical products together—especially never combine chlorine-based products with acids or ammonia. If you smell strong fumes or feel dizzy, step away from the pool and ventilate immediately.

  • Brush before you treat—mechanical removal exposes cells that chemicals can't reach
  • Run the pump 24/7 during treatment so dead cells get filtered out
  • Test free chlorine and pH each morning for five consecutive days after treatment
  • If black algae keeps returning in the same spot, the plaster may be pitted; get a professional resurfacing quote

Don't Skip the Filter

All that dead algae ends up in your filter. A cartridge clogged with organic matter recirculates particles right back into the water, undoing your work. Backwash a sand or DE filter as soon as the pressure gauge rises about 25 % above its clean baseline. For cartridge filters, pull them out, hose from inside out, and soak in a filter-cleaner solution if they look grimy.

In many Sanur villas the filter sits outdoors under a carport or on the terrace. After a heavy rain, check the strainer basket and air vent for debris before restarting the pump.

Prevention That Actually Works

The single most effective habit is consistent circulation. Run your pump at least eight hours a day year-round; during the rainy season bump it to twelve to clear the extra organic load. Keep free chlorine between 3–5 ppm and pH at 7.2–7.6—test twice a week minimum.

Cover the pool when you're not using it. In Sanur's humidity an uncovered pool picks up dust, pollen, and salt spray within a day. A fitted cover also keeps leaves out, which means less food for algae. If your villa has a rooftop tank feeding the top-up line, make sure the overflow isn't dumping stagnant water back into the basin—that's a quiet algae factory.

  • Test water every two weeks even when the pool looks clear
  • Brush tiles weekly in shaded corners where black algae likes to hide
  • Replace cartridges or backwash sand/DE units on schedule, not just when clogged
  • After heavy rain, do a quick visual check of the waterline and skimmer area

Questions

How long does it take to kill mustard algae completely?

Most mustards respond within three to five days of consistent brushing, algaecide application, and continuous filtration. If it reappears after that window, the biofilm is thicker than expected and a second full treatment cycle is usually needed.

Can I use vinegar or baking soda instead of commercial algicide?

Those household products won't penetrate the biofilm layer protecting both black and mustard algae. You risk upsetting pH without actually killing the cells. Stick with a proper pool-grade algaecide and follow the label directions.

My pool was fine all dry season but turned green after the first rains—why?

Rainwater carries organic matter, lowers alkalinity, and dilutes your chlorine. In Sanur the downpours are intense and short, so leaf litter and dust hit the surface in minutes. A post-rain water test and a light shock dose will usually reset things before algae gets a foothold.
